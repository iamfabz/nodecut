# Type — words on screen

In a code-drawn film the type is not a caption layer, it is a drawing like any other. It has the same problems: contrast,
timing, weight, and the question of whether it should be there at all.

## Before setting a word

Ask whether the beat wants text. A line of copy is an admission that the picture cannot carry the idea alone. That is
often true and fine — but a film where every beat is a caption is a film where nothing was designed.

Text earns its place when:

- it is **the idea** (`kinetic_type` is a medium, not a label),
- it **names** something the picture cannot (a brand, a price, a claim),
- it **measures** something (a number, a count, a comparison).

It does not earn its place when it describes what you can already see.

## Sizes at 1080p

| role | size | why |
|---|---|---|
| brand / end card | 64–96 px, weight 300–400, tracking 0.6–0.8 em | whispers; it is looked at longest |
| claim | 40–56 px, weight 500–600 | the boldest line, but not the biggest |
| supporting line | 20–24 px, weight 400–500 | must still be readable at 640 px wide |
| label / index / timecode | 11–13 px, weight 500, tracking 0.4 em | furniture; invisible unless looked for |

**Never below 19 px** for anything a viewer must read. **Never above ~100 px** — a giant word is a shout, and a shout is
what templates do.

## Tracking

Generous letter-spacing reads as expensive. Tight tracking reads as a tech product.

| feel | tracking |
|---|---|
| luxury, editorial | 0.5–0.8 em |
| modern brand | 0.3–0.5 em |
| technical, data | 0.2–0.3 em |
| a shout | 0 em — avoid |

## Two voices

Use at most two families, with a clear job split:

- **display** — the emotional voice: the brand, the claim.
- **machine** — the factual voice: numbers, labels, timecode, code. A mono or a neutral grotesque.

Three families is a mess. One family in two weights can be enough for a short film.

## The scrim

Text over a photograph needs separation or it will be unreadable on some frames and invisible on others.

- A **soft elliptical falloff** behind the line reads as depth of field. A hard rectangle reads as a pasted caption box and
  the eye finds its edges before the words.
- Aim for **contrast ≥ 200:1 luma range** in the band where the copy sits (measured, not guessed).
- Do not put text on a busy area and hope. Move the text, or move the picture.

## Kinetic entrances

Static type in a moving film looks dead. Every line should arrive:

| entrance | feel | use |
|---|---|---|
| rise + settle | clean, modern | most lines |
| per-letter cascade | alive, expensive | a brand name, a headline |
| brush / wipe fill | crafted, print-like | when the line is the whole beat |
| shear-straighten | energetic | short claims |
| mask reveal from behind a rule | editorial | section headings |

The rule for all of them: **a line must finish arriving at least 0.5 s before the cut.** An unfinished line is a mistake the
viewer sees.

## When type is the beat

For `kinetic_type`, the type *is* the image, so:

- make it **large** — this is the one place a 100 px word is right,
- **stack** it rather than running one long line; three short words beat a sentence,
- push the picture behind it almost to black so it reads as texture, not as a competing subject,
- give each word its own arrival, so the beat assembles instead of appearing.

## Accessibility

- Minimum contrast **WCAG AA** against what is actually behind it, not against black.
- Do not rely on colour alone to distinguish anything.
- Read every line out loud once. If it does not fit in the beat's duration, cut words, not time.

## Furniture

Small labels in the corners — a section index, a running timecode, a "01 / 06" — make a frame read as *designed*. They cost
almost nothing and fill the dead space that otherwise makes a film look empty. Keep them 11–13 px and put them where they
never compete with the copy.
