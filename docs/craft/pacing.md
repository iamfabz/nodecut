# Pacing — how long a beat holds, and when something must change

Short films fail on pacing more often than on any other single thing. A beat that holds too long is the most common
note a viewer leaves, and the hardest to see while you are making it — you already know what it says, so it feels fast.

## The numbers

| rule | value | why |
|---|---|---|
| a new visual element | every **1–3 s** in short social cuts, every **3–5 s** in a film | below 3 s the eye has read the frame and is waiting |
| a beat (a unit with its own purpose) | **2–4 s** for an ad, **5–8 s** for a film | shorter cannot earn a payoff, longer needs internal change |
| pattern interrupt | every **45–90 s** in long pieces | resets attention; irrelevant under 30 s |
| deliberate silence | **1–3 s** after a key line | the ear needs a gap to register that it was important |
| a text beat readable | **min 1.2 s**, ideally 1.5 s+ | under 1.2 s nobody finishes reading it |
| hold after the last arrival | **0.5–1.5 s** | a beat must land *before* the cut or the cut eats the line |

The last row is the one people skip. **If the copy is still assembling when the beat cuts, the beat failed.** Time the
arrival, then hold.

## Inside a beat

A 3-second beat is not one event. It is usually three:

```
0.0 – 0.3   the frame changes: the shot arrives, a band lifts, a wash
0.3 – 1.6   the content arrives: type resolves, a bar climbs, a word is filled
1.6 – 3.0   it is held and the frame is alive: a slow push, a moving highlight, a ticking counter
```

If nothing occupies the third section, the beat is dead for half its length. Add a slow camera move, a light that travels,
a number that counts, a rule that draws itself. Something has to be alive even when nothing is happening.

## Building rhythm

**Vary duration.** Beats of exactly 3.33 s each, repeated six times, are a metronome. Most films want one beat noticeably
shorter (a snap, 1.5 s) and one noticeably longer (the landing, 5 s).

**Vary the entrance.** If every beat enters by fading in, the film has one gesture. Rotate: a hard cut, a white burn, a
band that lifts, a wipe that reveals, a shape that grows into the next shot.

**Cut on movement.** The best cut lands while something is still moving. Cutting on a still frame is what makes an edit
feel like a slideshow even when the shots are good.

**Hold the last beat.** The final beat should be the quietest and the longest. It is the only one the viewer gets to sit
with.

## Silence and timing

- Leave **one beat of near-stillness** somewhere in the middle. A film that never stops moving has no shape.
- Do not put a cut where a line is being read. Read the copy out loud on the timeline; if you cannot finish it before the
  cut, the beat is too short or the line is too long.
- The first 1–2 seconds decide whether anyone watches the rest. Open on something that is already happening.

## A worked 20-second shape

| beat | length | what changes inside it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 establish | 3.3 s | bars slide away revealing the field; a line lands over it |
| 2 subject | 3.3 s | push in; a name resolves; a rule draws under it |
| 3 idea | 3.3 s | the words assemble in three arrivals, then hold with a sweep |
| 4 product | 3.3 s | a ring draws around the object; the lockup assembles |
| 5 claim | 3.3 s | a bar climbs; a number ticks; the line holds |
| 6 landing | 3.5 s | everything assembles, then a slow fade out |

Six beats, one internal change each, one held landing. Nothing is on screen for more than 3.5 s without something moving.

## Checking it

Take a contact sheet of the film at even intervals. If two frames look like the same beat, the pacing is wrong — either
the beat is too long or the change inside it is invisible. That check takes a second and catches the failure a viewer would
otherwise leave as a note.
